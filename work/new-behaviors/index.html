Introduction — Losing my place (1½ min)

Working on images, I iterate a lot, and I follow many different branches of the exploration at once.

But Midjourney only shows it as a huge grid, and I lose my place.

Variation is a tree. An image has children; one of them becomes the new parent; three of its children branch again. By the end I've followed the tree twenty ways.

Then I only ever see it chronologically, every branch snipped off and lined up, and I have to hold the whole structure in my head to know how I got to an image.

I've been interested in a lot of things, especially image generation recently. If you're like me, you use Midjourney a lot, and then your computer slows down when you try to zoom out.

But the Midjourney UI only shows it in a huge grid, and I end up losing my place a lot. Interlude — Moving through latent space (2½ min)

GANBreeder ran BigGAN, trained on ImageNet's categories. You could ask how much peacock, and maybe some negative alligator. It was my first real encounter with latent space.

But you could only drag one slider at a time. With Nama Tiwari at CMU, I built a realtime version, Xoromancy: each hand's six degrees of freedom drive twelve dimensions of the latent vector at once.

It was the first time I felt what it's like to phase myself through latent space and get articulate feedback from the system.

We train these models on gargantuan amounts of data, and then the space is far too large to ever explore, especially one slider at a time.

Today one prompt gives one image, or four. DALL·E 2's beta gave ten, and ten sample points let you feel the variance of a prompt, like everyone feeling the elephant.

I want that expressiveness back for today's image and video models. The challenge is the semantic mapping between inputs and outputs.

I'd like to talk more about latent space exploration in general. If you remember Artbreeder, or GANBreeder back in the day, in 2018 and 2019, they were running BigGAN. BigGAN was trained on ImageNet, so it had about a thousand different categories, and you could adjust how much peacock is in this, maybe some negative alligator, and you could sort of see it.

But I could really only drag one slider at a time, and I'd have to let up, and I was still having to wait. It was still incredible, and it was my first real encounter with latent space at all. But I really wanted to move multiple sliders at the same time, so I worked with my friend Nama Tiwari at CMU on a realtime version of this.

What we ended up building is called Xoromancy. We took the six degrees of freedom, rotation and translation, of each hand, so just twelve, and mapped those to the weights of the first twelve components of the thousand-dimensional Z vector, which controls the composition, not the category. This was the first time I was really encountering what it feels like to phase myself through latent space and get more articulate feedback from the system.

The hard part is that we train these models on gargantuan amounts of data, but then it's nearly impossible to find anything, because the space is way too large to ever actually explore, especially if you're only ever exploring it one slider at a time.

I yearn for the days we can get to this level of expressiveness with latent space in more LLM-based image models and video models. The challenge then is: what's the semantic mapping between your inputs and the outputs?

Demo 4 — Bound to the representation (1½ min)

The hard part: you're still reliant on the base representation.

To open its gills, there's no handle. It's vector shapes made to look nice by a style transfer.

The hard part, though, is that you're still reliant on what the base representation is. For example, some people at tldraw have been working on this canvas tool. The underlying representation of this fish is an ellipse, a triangle, another triangle. If you want to animate the fish, you're bound to the affordances of that underlying canvas.

If I want to go in and actually open up the gills, there's really no handle in this model, because all it really is is some vector shapes. It looks nice because of the style transfer.

I think this is my main issue with a lot of these AI tools: you can only operate them on the underlying, more classical representation, instead of operating in the world model of the latent space itself. Hopefully these more realtime models will actually have an understanding of, oh, you generated a fin, and build a 3D gizmo around it, and fully adapt to the dynamics you're going for.
